# I. Foundations

## 01 — Practice Overview

### Purpose

This document defines the research philosophy, visual language, communication principles, and design standards that guide the practice of Skye Reich.

It serves as the primary reference for digital and physical expressions of the practice, including websites, publications, exhibitions, presentations, research documentation, products, installations, and future collaborations.

This manual is written for both human collaborators and AI-assisted design systems. It should be reviewed before generating any page, component, interaction, or written content.

### Practice Statement

This practice investigates how biological systems, ecological relationships, and material science can inform regenerative approaches to design.

Research begins through observation of natural systems and develops through experimentation, fabrication, documentation, and long-term evaluation.

Materials are understood as participants within larger ecological cycles whose relationships continue beyond fabrication and human use.

The objective is not simply to produce objects, but to develop material systems, environments, and design methods that participate within living ecological processes.

## 02 — Mission

To investigate regenerative approaches to design through biological observation, material experimentation, ecological relationships, artistic inquiry, and fabrication.

## 03 — Vision

To contribute to a future in which design functions as an active participant within ecological systems rather than existing outside them.

Long-term objectives include:

- Developing locally responsive regenerative materials
- Creating ecological public artworks
- Designing products that provide environmental benefits after human use
- Consulting on packaging and products made from abundant, local, or invasive materials
- Integrating seeds, soil support, water filtration, habitat, and other ecological functions into designed systems
- Strengthening relationships between people, materials, communities, and environments

## 04 — Core Philosophy

### Humans as Participants

Humans are not separate from ecological systems. Every material extracted, transformed, used, and discarded continues participating within biological, geological, and climatic processes.

### Nature as Collaborator

Nature is approached as an active collaborator rather than a decorative reference or passive resource.

### Relationships Before Objects

The primary subject of the practice is the relationship an object reveals, strengthens, or creates between organisms, materials, landscapes, communities, climate, culture, and time.

### Process as Evidence

Observation, experimentation, failure, refinement, fabrication, installation, weathering, ecological interaction, and long-term evaluation are equally important stages of research.

### Growth Over Completion

Projects are evolving investigations rather than fixed artifacts. Every project has an afterlife.

## 05 — Design Principles

1. **Reveal relationships.**  
   Graphics, photography, writing, diagrams, and interaction should expose relationships that may otherwise remain unseen.

2. **Follow growth logic.**  
   Visual organization may draw from branching, venation, hyperbolic growth, river systems, coral formation, fungal networks, layering, and succession.

3. **Increase complexity through exploration.**  
   Initial encounters remain clear and accessible. Greater detail appears as the visitor moves deeper into the site.

4. **Treat research with visual care.**  
   Sketches, prototypes, failures, field notes, microscopy, measurements, and tests deserve the same attention as finished outcomes.

5. **Document the afterlife.**  
   Project narratives should continue through use, weathering, maintenance, ecological interaction, decomposition, and future research.

6. **Let the work provide richness.**  
   The interface provides clarity; materials, imagery, texture, and research provide visual energy.

## 06 — Practice Characteristics

The practice should feel:

- Curious
- Rigorous
- Experimental
- Regenerative
- Interdisciplinary
- Timeless
- Optimistic
- Intentional
- A little strange
- Always evolving

It should never feel:

- Greenwashed
- Corporate
- Sterile
- Trend-driven
- Fake
- Forced
- Static
- Focused only on final outcomes

## 07 — Audience

Primary audiences include:

- Artists
- Scientists
- Conservationists
- Museums and cultural institutions
- Material researchers
- Universities
- Public art organizations
- Designers and architects
- Environmentally focused companies
- Grant organizations

Communication should remain scientifically grounded while accessible to non-specialists.

## 08 — Success Criteria

The system succeeds when visitors understand that people, materials, objects, and environments already exist within the same ecological systems.

The website should communicate that design can participate in, strengthen, and extend those relationships.

# II. Visual Identity & Language

## Purpose

The visual identity translates the practice into a consistent visual system.

The identity emerges through natural patterns, material textures, botanical and scientific references, organized complexity, and careful documentation rather than through decorative branding.

## 2.1 Visual Experience

The website should feel like a combination of:

- A botanical encyclopedia
- A natural history field notebook
- An artist monograph
- A material library
- A conservatory with outdoor space
- Research rooms containing tests, failures, notes, samples, and prototypes
- A living scientific archive

The visitor should feel:

- Curious
- Interested
- Familiar with the natural references
- Surprised by their application
- Invited to explore
- Hopeful
- Intellectually engaged

The experience should feel timeless rather than conventionally futuristic.

## 2.2 Information Density

Information should increase with exploration.

1. Homepage
2. Project overview
3. Research context
4. Experiments
5. Process documentation
6. Microscopic or technical detail
7. Final outcome
8. Afterlife
9. Future research

A visitor should understand the main idea quickly, while deeper exploration should reveal substantially more information.

- Scientific illustration
- Taxonomic organization
- Field notebooks

### Material Libraries

### Conservatories and Landscapes

- Warm light through tree canopies
- Reflected light over water

## 2.4 Natural Systems

Primary references:

- Coral

Secondary references:

- Network formation
- Self-similarity
- Gradients of complexity

Fractal systems may be abstract in some areas and visually prominent in others, similar to changing magnification within a Mandelbrot set.

Priority order:

1. Texture
2. Scale
3. Process
4. Environmental context
5. Human interaction
6. Microscopic detail
7. Scientific documentation
8. Finished outcome

Preferred images include:

- Hands working
- Material tests
- Imperfect prototypes
- Environmental context
- Installation
- Public interaction
- Weathering
- Decomposition

## 2.10 Lighting

Preferred:

- Warm light through tree canopies
- Diffuse forest light
- Soft overcast light
- Morning mist
- Sun rays through clouds
- Reflected light from water

Avoid:

- Harsh flash
- High-contrast commercial studio lighting
- Artificial colored lighting
- Overly theatrical effects

## 2.11 Composition

Composition balances structured editorial organization with complex organic content.

Use:

- Large negative space around dense imagery
- Collections rather than isolated objects
- Repetition with meaningful variation
- Alternation between open and dense sections
- Macro and micro views
- Scale changes that communicate purpose
- Clear visual pathways
- Calm page structure supporting expressive material content

Hierarchy:

1. Material or project imagery
2. Research relationships
3. Archive metadata
4. Diagrams and supporting evidence
5. Written explanation

Pages should move through:

**Observation → Explanation → Discovery → Reflection**

# III. Typography

## Purpose

Typography provides clarity and structure. It supports the research rather than competing with it.

## Principles

- Editorial rather than corporate
- Scientific without feeling clinical
- Elegant and highly readable
- Consistent across projects
- Hierarchy through scale and spacing rather than decoration

## Typeface Roles

| Role | Typeface | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Display | Canela | Page titles and section headings |
| Editorial | Cormorant Garamond | Introductions and quotations |
| Interface | IBM Plex Sans | Navigation and body text |
| Scientific | IBM Plex Mono | Archive IDs, captions, measurements, dates, and metadata |

### Font Availability Rule

If Canela is unavailable or cannot be licensed, use a refined editorial serif with organic curves and strong readability. Do not substitute a generic high-contrast fashion serif without testing it against the visual system.

## Rules

- Keep typography restrained.
- Let photography and materials carry visual weight.
- Use metadata consistently.
- Maintain generous line spacing.
- Keep body text easy to read.
- Do not use monospace for paragraphs.
- Avoid excessive font weights.
- Avoid decorative typography.
- Avoid using many text colors.

# IV. Color

## Purpose

The palette is derived from naturally occurring materials. It provides warmth and structure while allowing project imagery to supply the strongest color.

## Primary Palette

| Token | Name | Hex | Use |
|---|---|---:|---|
| `--color-paper` | Archive Paper | `#F5F1E8` | Primary background |
| `--color-ink` | Archive Ink | `#232321` | Primary text |
| `--color-moss` | Moss | `#5C6B58` | Links and ecological accents |
| `--color-stone` | Stone | `#8E8A82` | Secondary text and dividers |
| `--color-amber` | Amber Resin | `#C18A4A` | Select highlights |
| `--color-mineral` | Mineral Blue | `#6C8791` | Diagrams and data graphics |

## Color Rules

- Use warm neutral backgrounds.
- Let photography provide most of the color.
- Use accent colors sparingly.
- Maintain accessible contrast.
- Avoid pure black and pure white where practical.
- Avoid neon colors.
- Avoid highly saturated interface colors.
- Avoid synthetic or technology-focused gradients.
- Iridescent color may appear only when tied to a material reference and used with restraint.

# V. Layout

## Purpose

The layout system organizes research into a clear, exploratory experience.

## Core Structure

Project pages should generally follow:

1. Hero image or video
2. Archive metadata
3. Project overview
4. Research question
5. Environmental context
6. Material studies
7. Process and experiments
8. Observations and findings
9. Final outcome
10. Afterlife
11. Future research
12. References or attached documents

Not every project requires every section, but each page should tell a complete research story.

## Layout Rules

- Use generous space around detailed imagery.
- Alternate immersive media with smaller evidence-based collections.
- Use collections to show process and iteration.
- Move between landscape, system, object, material, texture, and microscopic scale.
- Prioritize imagery over interface.
- Keep long-form documents attached rather than placing multi-page essays directly on the website.
- Use organic visual pathways without making navigation confusing.
- Ensure the destination of links and pathways is visible before interaction.

# IX. Graphics and Data Visualization

## Diagram Types

Preferred:

- Cross sections
- Material lifecycle diagrams
- Ecological relationship maps
- Growth sequences
- Process diagrams
- Layer diagrams
- Branching networks
- Decomposition timelines
- Measurement overlays

## Diagram Style

- Thin lines
- Clear labels
- Restrained color
- Botanical or scientific illustration influence
- A balance between poetic and technical
- No technology-dashboard styling

## Data Visualization

When presenting measurements such as soil pH, germination, strength, humidity, decomposition, or duration:

- Use simple two-dimensional charts.
- Use approved palette colors.
- Label units clearly.
- Distinguish observation from interpretation.
- Avoid unnecessary 3D effects.
- Avoid decorative chart elements.
- Include methodology or sample context where relevant.

# X. Motion

## Purpose

Motion should support discovery and reflect growth logic.

## Preferred Behaviors

- Reveal
- Expand
- Branch
- Unfold
- Fade
- Layer
- Move gradually between scales

## Rules

- Motion should never obstruct access to information.
- Navigation may subtly follow branching or river-like pathways.
- Background fractals may move slowly or respond to scroll.
- Use more visible pattern behavior in selected immersive areas and subtler pattern behavior in reading areas.
- Avoid bouncing.
- Avoid spinning.
- Avoid exaggerated parallax.
- Avoid abrupt transitions.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences.

# XI. Responsive and Accessible Design

## Responsive Rules

### Desktop

- Use multi-column compositions where helpful.
- Allow immersive media and side-by-side comparisons.
- Maintain visible hierarchy.

### Tablet

- Reduce columns while preserving relationships.
- Keep captions and metadata close to their associated media.

### Mobile

- Use a clear single-column structure.
- Do not remove essential research.
- Simplify motion before simplifying content.
- Preserve readable captions and metadata.
- Ensure navigation remains direct.

## Accessibility Requirements

- Meet WCAG AA contrast standards.
- Use semantic HTML.
- Support keyboard navigation.
- Provide meaningful alt text.
- Associate captions with images.
- Maintain readable text sizes.
- Support reduced motion.
- Avoid conveying meaning through color alone.
- Use descriptive link text.

# XIV. AI Implementation Instructions

## Required Reading

Before generating or modifying any page, component, interaction, copy, or visual system:

1. Read this document in full.
2. Preserve all established design tokens and component rules.
3. Reuse existing patterns before inventing new ones.
4. Ask only for information that cannot be inferred from this system or supplied content.

## Primary Objective

Build a digital research environment, not a conventional portfolio.

The website should reveal an evolving body of work through material evidence, environmental context, process, experimentation, final outcomes, and afterlife.

## Core Design Logic

Every design decision should do at least one of the following:

- Reveal a relationship
- Organize complexity
- Document research
- Support observation
- Guide exploration
- Show growth or transformation
- Strengthen understanding

Remove elements that do none of these.

## Visual Direction

The site should feel:

- Editorial
- Scientific
- Organic
- Warm
- Intentional
- Tactile
- Timeless
- Experimental
- A little strange

The site should not feel:

- Corporate
- Minimalist for its own sake
- Like a technology startup
- Like a SaaS dashboard
- Like an environmental nonprofit template
- Like a luxury fashion storefront
- Cyberpunk
- Artificially futuristic
- Greenwashed

## Interface Priority

Prioritize in this order:

1. The work
2. Material behavior and texture
3. Environmental and human relationships
4. Research process
5. Evidence and diagrams
6. Metadata
7. Typography
8. Interface decoration

The interface is a guide and curator. It is not the exhibit.

## Homepage Instruction

The homepage should begin with a large video or animated growth pattern.

It should provide clear pathways into the archive without exposing all information at once.

Navigation may feel river-like, branching, or growth-based, but visitors must always understand where a path leads.

Do not create a generic hero with a centered slogan and oversized call-to-action button.

## Layout Instruction

- Alternate open editorial sections with dense research collections.
- Use large imagery for experience, medium imagery for process, and small imagery for evidence.
- Move between macro and micro scales.
- Use white space to frame detail.
- Avoid clutter.
- Avoid rigid symmetry throughout the entire site.
- Do not hide essential information behind decorative interactions.

## Typography Instruction

- Use the approved typeface roles.
- Keep body text highly readable.
- Keep metadata concise and consistent.
- Do not make typography visually louder than the work.
- Do not introduce additional fonts without updating this document.

## Color Instruction

- Use approved color tokens.
- Let photography supply the strongest colors.
- Use Moss, Amber Resin, and Mineral Blue selectively.
- Do not introduce neon colors, saturated gradients, or pure black-and-white branding.
- Iridescence may be used only as a restrained material reference.

## Graphics Instruction

Use:

- Botanical illustration
- Cross sections
- Measurement marks
- Growth rings
- Dendritic structures
- Hyperbolic forms
- Layer diagrams
- Specimen labels
- Scientific diagrams
- Microscopy
- Research sketches

Avoid:

- AI blobs
- Circuit-board graphics
- Generic leaf icons
- DNA helixes
- Hexagonal technology patterns
- HUD interfaces
- Stock icon packs
- Decorative graphics with no informational purpose

## Photography Instruction

Prioritize texture, scale, process, environmental context, human interaction, and afterlife.

Do not replace missing project photography with generic stock images.

People should appear as participants in systems rather than as the primary subject.

## Motion Instruction

Motion should reveal, unfold, branch, expand, or gradually change scale.

Do not use bouncing, spinning, abrupt movement, or animation solely to create spectacle.

Always provide reduced-motion behavior.

## Project Page Instruction

Whenever content is available, include:

1. Archive metadata
2. Overview
3. Research question
4. Environmental context
5. Materials
6. Process
7. Experiments
8. Observations
9. Findings
10. Final outcome
11. Afterlife
12. Future research
13. References or downloads

Do not reduce a project to a hero image, short description, and gallery.

## Writing Instruction

- Use neutral, scientific language.
- Be concise.
- Describe observations before conclusions.
- Avoid marketing claims.
- Avoid overstating environmental benefits.
- Clearly identify uncertainty.
- Use consistent archive terminology.

## Accessibility Instruction

Accessibility is mandatory and should not be treated as a visual compromise.

Use semantic HTML, keyboard support, meaningful alt text, readable contrast, descriptive links, and reduced-motion support.

## Decision Framework

When multiple solutions are possible, choose the one that:

1. Makes the research easier to understand
2. Keeps the work visually prominent
3. Encourages curiosity
4. Preserves the established system
5. Avoids temporary design trends
6. Supports future archive growth

## Final Instruction

Do not design for novelty alone.

Build a clear, evolving digital archive in which complexity emerges through exploration and beauty comes from the relationship between natural systems, material research, evidence, and careful organization.
